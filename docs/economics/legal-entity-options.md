# Legal entity options

*Research notes only — not legal advice. A lawyer familiar with cooperatives must be consulted before choosing.*

The Union needs a legal entity to sign contracts, hold IP, receive revenue, and pay members across many countries.

## Requirements
- Worker/member ownership and democratic governance
- Can pay members internationally (employees, contractors, or both)
- Can hold IP and sign client and sponsor contracts
- Reasonable cost and admin overhead for an early-stage group
- Allows adding hundreds of members over time

## Options to investigate

| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **EU worker cooperative** (e.g., Spain's *cooperativa de trabajo*, Italy, France's SCOP/SCIC) | Native co-op law, strong worker-ownership tradition | Members often must be residents/employees; heavy for international contractors |
| **European Cooperative Society (SCE)** | EU-wide co-op form | Requires members in ≥ 2 EU countries; complex, rarely used |
| **Estonian OÜ (via e-Residency) with co-op bylaws** | Fully online, cheap, international-friendly | Not a legal co-op; co-op rules must be contractual |
| **US LLC with cooperative operating agreement** (e.g., Colorado/Delaware) | Flexible, well understood by clients and payment processors | US tax complexity for foreign members |
| **UK Co-operative Society / CIC** | Mature co-op law | Post-Brexit EU friction |
| **Platform cooperative + Employer-of-Record** | Entity in one country, EOR/contractor platform pays members globally | EOR fees; co-op rights must be contractual |

## Recommended next step
Shortlist 2–3 options based on where the founding members live, then get a paid consultation with a cooperative-law specialist. Track the decision in [Open questions](../open-questions.md).
